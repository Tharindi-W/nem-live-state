01What the feeds actually expose

You were using three feeds and reading maybe half of what they return. The dashboard now reads the rest, and the collector archives four more.

The dispatch summary was the biggest surprise. It was being parsed for price and demand while three fields sat untouched in every response:

  • PRICE_STATUS, APCFLAG, MARKETSUSPENDEDFLAG — the regime markers. They are constant almost always, which is exactly why they matter: they flag the rare intervals where the market stops pricing freely, and those are the intervals a forecast has to survive.
  • INTERCONNECTORFLOWS — a nested JSON string carrying, for each of the six links, the flow and both directional limits. Flow against limit is constraint-binding proximity. That is the network state regional price separation depends on, and it was being discarded every five minutes.

The pre-dispatch feed hid something better. Every call returns a full trailing day of five-minute actuals — 5 regions × 288 intervals — and the collector was using them only to derive a timestamp before throwing them away. Archiving them lifted the forecast-to-actual match rate from roughly 10% to near complete.

Rooftop solar is the one nobody meters

Behind-the-meter solar does not appear in TOTALDEMAND — that field is operational demand, and Australia has the highest per-capita rooftop PV on earth at roughly 26 GW inside the NEM. AEMO cannot measure it; it is estimated from satellite imagery plus a sampled subset of monitored sites.

Which means AEMO publishes two independent actuals streams for the same interval: a SATELLITE estimate and a MEASUREMENT series. The gap between them is a free, self-labelling estimate-versus-truth pair. First reading from the archive, at the morning ramp:

Licensing trap

The report treats Bureau of Meteorology feeds as a free data source and ranks weather as the second-best domain to build on.

BoM products are free to access but explicitly not licensed for commercial use. Commercial use requires a paid data licence. Open-Meteo's convenient bom_access_global endpoint redistributes exactly that data — so the dashboard pins ecmwf_ifs025 instead. Resolve this before you charge anyone anything.

02Why forecasting is hard here specifically

Not "electricity is volatile." Four structural facts about this market that make it a different problem from forecasting demand.

The price is a dual variable

Demand is a physical quantity with continuous dynamics and bounded derivatives — you can forecast it to a couple of percent. Price is the shadow price of the regional energy balance constraint in a constrained optimisation. It has no inertia, no conservation law, no smoothness. It can move from −$50 to the cap in three hundred seconds without any physical variable moving more than a few percent, because a constraint bound.

Forecasting demand and forecasting price are not the same discipline. Most teams that fail here fail because they treat them as one.

The dynamic range is 24,200 to one

The floor is −$1,000/MWh and the market price cap is $23,200/MWh for FY2026-27. That span reprices 288 times a day. Negative prices are not an anomaly — in Q1 2026 they were 31% of all South Australian intervals and around two-thirds of SA daytime intervals. Any chart with a linear price axis is lying, which is why the dashboard uses a symlog scale throughout.

The tail is the market

Twenty-eight intervals out of 25,920 — one tenth of one percent — produced roughly 29% of South Australia's quarterly average price. AEMO Quarterly Energy Dynamics, Q1 2026

In that quarter SA averaged $88/MWh, of which $38 — 43% — came from intervals above $300. A single day, 26 January, contributed about 29% of the whole quarter. This is why point forecasts are close to worthless: a model trained on absolute error produces conditional-median forecasts, and the conditional median essentially never spikes. It is mathematically incapable of predicting the events that generate the returns.

The generating process changed last year

Between Q1 2025 and Q1 2026 the NEM added 4,445 MW / 11,219 MWh of grid-scale battery, more than doubling installed capacity. Batteries now set the price in 32–36% of dispatch intervals, up from about 17%, displacing hydro as the most frequent price-setting technology.

That is not a change in the level of prices. It is a change in the mechanism. And it brings a hidden state variable with it: battery state of charge determines how long a spike lasts, because discharge sets price until SOC depletes and then hands off to gas. A model without SOC cannot get spike duration right — and duration is exactly what determines a battery's revenue.

A second, sharper example of regime change: in Q1 2025, 12% of negative prices sat in the −$10 to $0 band. In Q1 2026 that was 68%. The cause was not physics — large-scale generation certificate prices collapsed from $29 to $5, changing every renewable generator's economic floor bid. No amount of architecture catches that. Only an exogenous feature does.

03Is this a chaotic system?

Asked rigorously, the answer is no — and the reason is more useful than the label would have been.

There is a real literature here, and its most instructive feature is that it corrected itself. A 2016 paper found positive maximal Lyapunov exponents across energy commodity series and concluded they were deterministic rather than stochastic. In 2022 the same authors re-ran it with noise-level estimation and found negative exponents for most series and noise levels of 40–58% — meaning the "attractor" was mostly noise ball. A study of Greek and Italian electricity prices using the noise-robust Jacobian method found λmax negative throughout: nonlinear dependence, yes; chaos, no.

The useful move is to stop asking about the system as a whole and separate the layers, because they have different answers:

Layer 1
Weather — genuinely chaotic
The canonical Lorenz system. Synoptic error-doubling time of 1.5–2 days, practical limit around two weeks; mesoscale convection predictable only hours ahead. This is the only layer where "Lyapunov time" means anything, and it dominates all exogenous uncertainty beyond about an hour.
Layer 2
Physical grid — engineered away from chaos
A stiff nonlinear system with genuine bifurcation structure, but it is not operated there. Frequency control, protection and security constraints exist precisely to suppress positive exponents. When it fails, the failure mode is cascading — combinatorial, not chaotic.
Layer 3
The dispatch engine — deterministic and memoryless
Every five minutes NEMDE solves a security-constrained economic dispatch. Given the inputs, the output is a deterministic function. It has no state and no trajectory. Price jumps are the discontinuity of an argmin, not exponential divergence — structural instability at a fold, and locally completely predictable if you know the bid stack.
Layer 4
Strategic bidding — reflexive
Participants rebid against AEMO's published forecast, quoting it verbatim in their stated reasons. Your forecast, adopted widely enough, changes what it forecasts.

So the honest description: a regime-switching jump process, driven by a chaotically-forced exogenous input, filtered through a deterministic discontinuous optimisation operator, with an adaptive strategic layer on top. Formally the closest standard object is a piecewise-deterministic Markov process whose "flow" is the solution map of a parametric optimisation.

Positioning

"We're applying AI to a chaotic system."

Do not say "chaotic" in front of a NEM trader. They will hear "we don't have a model," and on the technical merits they would be right. The accurate words are discontinuous and regime-switching — and they happen to describe something you can actually build against, which "chaotic" does not.

There is one genuinely important consequence. Because layer 3 is a deterministic optimisation, the correct architecture is learn the inputs, compute the dispatch. The constraint equations and bid stacks are public. Learning end-to-end what you could compute exactly throws away a free structural prior — and that is the most expensive mistake available to you.

04Where AI actually fits

World models: right idea, wrong headline

A world model here would be a learned latent simulator of grid state — demand and its drivers, per-unit availability and battery SOC, the offer stack, the binding constraint set, the weather field — rollable forward and conditionable on interventions. There are four places it genuinely beats direct forecasting:

  1. Coherent joint scenarios. You cannot make a battery dispatch decision from marginal quantiles. Optimal storage dispatch depends on the joint distribution of the price path. A generative rollout gives you sample paths with correct temporal dependence. This is the strongest honest argument.
  2. Counterfactuals. "What if this unit trips at 17:30 with SA already at LOR1?" No direct forecaster answers that — the input distribution has no such example. In February 2024 six transmission towers collapsed near Anakie and Loy Yang A tripped simultaneously. You cannot predict that. You must be able to price it within seconds of it happening.
  3. Constraint-aware rollouts, because the binding set is persistent and driven by outages and topology.
  4. Sample-efficient RL for bidding, which needs a simulator to train in.

And where it is overkill: short-horizon quantile forecasting, where gradient-boosted quantile regression on lagged prices plus AEMO's own forecast features will beat a latent world model and ship in a week.

The weather model is the real world model

Conditional on the bid stack (public, persistent), the network topology (published) and unit availability (published), nearly all remaining uncertainty at 1–48 hours is uncertainty about hub-height wind, irradiance and temperature. The grid layer above that is comparatively shallow and mostly known.

There is a genuine "why now" in this. GenCast-class models produce probabilistic weather ensembles in minutes on a single accelerator, where traditional 50-member ensembles need HPC and hours. Tail-focused price forecasting requires large ensembles — you cannot estimate a 99th-percentile price from a deterministic weather run. That economics just flipped, and it has a date on it.

Built

The dashboard's weather panel samples ECMWF at wind-farm and solar-farm coordinates, not city coordinates. Adelaide is 180 km from the Mid-North wind farms and often sits in the opposite wind regime — plotting "Adelaide wind speed" next to "SA price" teaches a relationship that isn't there. wind_speed_100m is the load-bearing variable; wind_speed_10m is the decoy.

LLMs: wrong for the headline job, right for one real job

As numeric forecasters, the evidence is bad. A NeurIPS 2024 paper ablated the leading LLM-based time-series forecasters and found that removing the language model entirely, or replacing it with a single basic attention layer, did not degrade performance and usually improved it. Add that an LLM forward pass per five-minute interval per region per quantile is economically absurd against a gradient-boosting inference measured in microseconds.

But this market has an unusually rich natural-language surface that almost nobody mines:

  • Market notices — around 145,000 of them, including price-affecting interventions announced in prose minutes to hours before they bite. A direction issued for voltage control in SA changes the conditional price distribution in a way no numeric model sees until the dispatch outcome reveals it.
  • Rebid explanations — participants are legally required to state why they changed their offer, with event, awareness and decision timestamps. On a single quiet Saturday: 1,154 rebids across 568 units, 2,096 carrying text. Order 10⁵–10⁶ per year, back to 2009. These are timestamped statements of belief and intent, aligned to the moment the offer stack moved. Nothing else gives you a "why" channel.
  • Constraint equations — semi-formal language mapping to a machine-usable graph of which constraints bind under which outage affecting which units.

Be honest about the limits: the rebid field is short and formulaic by regulatory design. The value is in classification, linking and aggregation at scale, not literary comprehension — which is good news, because it means a small fine-tuned classifier plus an LLM for the hard 5% is the right engineering and the cost is trivial.

One hard rule. Never let the LLM emit a number that reaches a customer. It produces features, structure and prose. The forecast comes from the calibrated statistical model.

05The moat question

This one changes the plan

The report's central operational instruction is "archive the live stream from day one," on the reasoning that public sites keep only short rolling windows. It is treated as the defensible asset.

AEMO's MMSDM archive publishes everything you are collecting — including the vintaged P5MIN and pre-dispatch forecast solutions, rooftop PV actuals and forecasts, bid stacks with rebid explanations, and dispatch constraints — back to 2009, free, in about 200 tables. Your careful vintage capture is architecturally correct and also already done for you, for seventeen years.

So the archive is a latency advantage, not a history advantage. A competitor can have seventeen years by Friday. Worse, a three-day archive contains no spike event at all, so it can only measure the easy middle of the distribution and is structurally blind to where the money is. Backfilling MMSDM moves you from "wait two years for a tail event" to "train tomorrow."

Not a moat

  • Fetching public APIs. All of it is free and broadly licensed.
  • A nice dashboard. WattSight already does live NEM price and weather for $149/month, explicitly positioned against enterprise tools costing $5,000–15,000/year. ez2view has had 23 years to build fifty-plus widgets.
  • A generic LLM wrapper. LLM-and-power-systems publications went from 1 in 2022 to 464 in 2025. Loud field, thin results.

Actually defensible

  1. A public, continuously-updated forecast-skill scoreboard. Pinball loss and CRPS against AEMO's own forecasts, per region, per horizon, per hour, updated daily. Nobody in Australia publishes this. It is cheap to build, it forces every competitor to answer "so what's your score?", and while AEMO's side can be backfilled by anyone, your own live track record cannot.
  2. The natural-language corpus and extraction pipeline, typed and linked to dispatch outcomes with a validated evaluation set. Free inputs; expensive, boring, compounding asset.
  3. Latency and correctness engineering — price revisions, intervention pricing, APC flags and suspension regimes. Most people get this wrong and quietly poison their own backtests.
  4. An economic evaluation harness that scores forecasts by realised battery P&L, not MAE. Customers buy dollars.

06Is it a good startup idea?

The intersection as stated — world models × LLMs × chaotic systems — is a buzzword collision. There is a real thesis underneath it.

Term by term: "chaotic" is technically wrong and commercially harmful. "World model" is substantively right but will be read as hype unless you say what you actually mean. "LLM" is right for one job and wrong for the headline job, and conflating the two is the fastest way to be dismissed by a technical buyer.

Probabilistic, weather-driven, constraint-aware scenario simulation of NEM dispatch — with a language layer that turns AEMO's unstructured operational record into features and explanations — scored publicly and continuously against AEMO's own forecasts. Every clause falsifiable

The market, with the bad news included

The good news is real: 2025 saw 1.9 GW / 4.9 GWh of storage commissioned in Australia, more than 2017–2024 combined; around 45 GW of grid-scale storage sits in the connection queue; the federal home battery program passed 500,000 installations in thirteen months.

The bad news is equally real and you must confront it before an investor does. NEM grid-scale battery revenues fell from $121k/MW/yr in January 2026 to $44k/MW/yr in March — the lowest since tracking began. Q2 2026 spot averaged $74/MWh, down 47% year-on-year. FCAS costs are down 61%. You would be selling forecasting alpha into a customer base whose per-MW revenue just fell around 65% in a quarter.

The honest bull case is the ratio, not the level. More assets multiplied by lower revenue per asset means more competition for a shrinking optimisation margin — which raises the marginal value of forecast skill per MW. When spreads are $200/MWh everyone makes money and nobody buys a forecast. When spreads are $60/MWh, capture rate is the whole business. Say that explicitly; the naive "big battery boom!" pitch will not survive contact with a customer's P&L.

The wedge

Not "all five regions, all horizons, a dashboard." That is a product that already exists for $149 a month. Start at:

South Australian evening-peak spike probability, 5 minutes to 6 hours ahead, sold as a calibrated probability plus a machine-generated explanation, benchmarked publicly against AEMO's own forecasts.

SA is where the volatility lives (43% of the quarterly average price in under 1% of intervals), it is the most constraint-dominated region so the physics is tractable, the incumbent's failure mode is documented, the customer set is concentrated and reachable, and every spike generates a case study for the scoreboard.

Next, in order

  1. Backfill MMSDM. Everything downstream is starved without tail events. This is the single highest-value action available.
  2. Write the problem sentence. The report's own step 1, still undone. Name the region, the target, the horizons, and the loss function — and specify which AEMO product you are beating, because P5MIN and PREDISPATCH are different things and the report conflates them.
  3. Publish the scoreboard. "How good is AEMO's forecast, really?" — by region, horizon and hour, free, methodology published. You do not need a model of your own to publish this, and it immediately makes you the reference point.
  4. Climb the baseline ladder in order — seasonal naive, then quantile gradient boosting on lagged prices plus AEMO forecast features plus weather. Only then consider deep or foundation models, and only as ensemble members.
  5. Then the dispatch-reproduction gate: can you reproduce yesterday's prices from yesterday's published inputs? If yes, you have a world model. If no, you do not — and it is much better to learn that in month three than month eighteen.

Write the kill criteria down now, while you are still objective. If at day 90 you cannot beat AEMO's short-horizon forecast at p < 0.05, you do not have a forecasting company — you have a data-infrastructure and forensics company, which is smaller but real. Do not spend year two adding a transformer.
